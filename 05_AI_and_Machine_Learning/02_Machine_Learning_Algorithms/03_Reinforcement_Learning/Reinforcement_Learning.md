# Reinforcement Learning

**Reinforcement learning (RL)** is learning to act by trial and error. An agent takes actions, the world responds with a new situation and a **reward**, and the agent gradually changes its behaviour to collect as much reward as possible over time. Nobody tells it the right action; it has to discover which actions lead to good outcomes, often many steps later.

The output of RL is a [[Policy]]: a rule mapping observations to actions. The problem it solves is formalised as a [[Markov_Decision_Process]].

> **Etymology.** *Reinforcement* comes from Latin *re-* (again) and *infortiare* (to strengthen). In the 1890s–1930s psychology of Thorndike and later Skinner, a *reinforcer* was anything that strengthened a behaviour that preceded it. Computer scientists borrowed the term in the 1980s, when Richard Sutton and Andrew Barto connected animal-learning theory to Bellman's dynamic programming. Their textbook, first published in 1998, named the field.

## How RL differs from other learning

| | Supervised learning | Imitation learning | Reinforcement learning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Training signal | Correct answer for each input | Expert's action for each observation | A scalar reward, often delayed |
| Data | Fixed dataset | Fixed demonstrations | Generated by the agent itself |
| Main difficulty | Generalisation | Errors compounding off the demonstrated path | Exploration and credit assignment |
| Robot example | Detecting objects | Copying a teleoperated folding motion | Learning to walk on rough terrain |

Two things make RL hard. **Exploration**: the agent must try things it has not tried, without knowing in advance which are worth it. **Credit assignment**: when a reward finally arrives, which of the hundreds of earlier actions deserve the credit?

## The objective

The agent maximises expected discounted return:

$$J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t\, r_t\Big]$$

where $\tau$ is a trajectory produced by running policy $\pi$ in the environment and $\gamma \in [0,1)$ is the discount factor.

## The three families

**1. Value-based.** Learn the action-value function $Q(s,a)$ and act greedily: $a = \arg\max_a Q(s,a)$. Q-learning updates toward the Bellman target:

$$Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\big[r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\big]$$

DQN (Mnih et al. 2013) did this with a deep network on raw Atari pixels. Value methods suit discrete actions; robots mostly have continuous ones.

**2. Policy-gradient.** Parameterise the policy $\pi_\theta$ and climb the gradient of $J$ directly:

$$\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, \hat{A}_t\big]$$

where $\hat{A}_t$ (the *advantage*) says how much better the action was than average. **PPO** (Schulman et al. 2017) clips each update so the policy never changes too much at once. It is simple, stable and parallelises well, which is why it trains most legged-robot controllers today.

**3. Actor-critic, off-policy.** Learn a policy (actor) and a value function (critic) together, and reuse old experience from a replay buffer. **SAC** (Haarnoja et al. 2018) adds an entropy bonus that keeps the policy exploring. Off-policy methods need far fewer samples, so they are preferred when training on a real robot, where every sample costs time and wear.

## Model-free and model-based

- **Model-free** methods (PPO, SAC) learn a policy or value directly from experience.
- **Model-based** methods first learn how the world behaves, $\hat{P}(s' \mid s, a)$, then plan or train inside that learned model. **DreamerV3** (Hafner et al. 2023) learns a [[World_Models|world model]] and trains its policy entirely on imagined rollouts. Model-based RL is more sample-efficient; its risk is exploiting errors in the model.

## Worked example: why reward design is hard

Task: teach a simulated arm to push a block to a target. A first reward:

$$r = -\lVert p_{\text{block}} - p_{\text{target}} \rVert$$

Reasonable, but the arm can learn to flick the block so it slides past the target. That is not "wrong" by this reward, just wasteful. Engineers add terms:

$$r = -\lVert p_{\text{block}} - p_{\text{target}} \rVert \;-\; 0.01\,\lVert a \rVert^2 \;-\; 0.1\,\lVert \dot{p}_{\text{block}} \rVert \;+\; 5 \cdot \mathbb{1}[\text{block within 2 cm}]$$

Each term fixes one behaviour and can create another. This **reward engineering** is often where most of the human effort in an RL project goes, and it is one reason [[Imitation_Learning]] became popular for manipulation: showing is easier than specifying.

## Why robotics needs simulation

PPO typically needs tens of millions of environment steps for a locomotion policy. At 50 steps per second, 50 million steps is $50 \times 10^6 / 50 = 10^6$ seconds: about 11.6 days of a real robot running non-stop, falling over for most of it. Instead, policies are trained in physics simulation on GPUs. Rudin et al. (2021) trained a quadruped to walk in minutes by running thousands of simulated robots in parallel. The policy is then transferred to hardware; see [[Reinforcement_Learning_for_Robots]] for how that gap is closed.

## State of the art (as of November 2026)

- **Locomotion is RL's clearest win.** Quadrupeds and humanoids from many labs and companies walk, run and recover from pushes using PPO-trained policies, transferred from simulation.
- **RL on top of imitation.** Large [[Vision-Language-Action_Models]] are pretrained by imitation, then refined with reward signals, either from humans, from task success, or from learned reward models. This mirrors how language models are fine-tuned with RL after pretraining.
- **GPU simulation keeps getting faster.** Isaac Lab 3.0 (generally available October 2026) runs one task API over several physics backends, including the open-source Newton engine; MuJoCo 4.0 (November 2026) and MuJoCo Warp target the same massively parallel training.
- **Training inside learned models.** Training policies inside video-based [[World_Models]] is an active research direction, not yet a standard tool.
